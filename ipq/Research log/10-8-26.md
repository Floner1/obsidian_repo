# Question, hypothesis and prediction
- original question to use as context: whether data-driven strategy reduces the role of driver skill in F1.
- question that actually gets answered: To what extent did the FIA's 2014 to 2016 ban on in-race driver coaching change the lap-time gap between teammates in F1?
- how they link: the instructions given in coaching is technically data driven as engineers and pit wall members use data from previous laps to suggest changes, but it is only 1 part of the original data driven strategy question 
- Hypothesis: Because of the coaching ban that was implemented from the 2014 Singapore Grand Prix (Round 14/19) to the 2016 Hungarian Grand Prix (Round 11/21), the gap between teammates will widen during the ban and will narrow when the ban gets lifted.
- The hypothesis is not supported if the median teammate gap from the 2014 Singapore Grand Prix to the 2016 Hungarian Grand Prix is not larger than the median from the 2014 Australian Grand Prix to the 2014 Italian Grand Prix by at least X percent, or if the 2016 German to Abu Dhabi median is not smaller than the ban median by at least X percent.
- Prediction: The the teammate gap as a percentage of lap time from the 2014 Singapore Grand Prix (Round 14/19) to the 2016 Hungarian Grand Prix (Round 11/21), inclusive, will be larger than in the 2014 Australian Grand Prix to the 2014 Italian Grand Prix and the 2016 German Grand Prix to the 2016 Abu Dhabi Grand Prix by at least 1% of the median lap time. 
- I use a percentage here because different tracks have different lengths. a 0.6 second gap on a 90 second a lap track is the same size relative to a 0.8second gap on 120 second a lap track. 
- The FIA's motive for this ban, as reported by Motorsports.com, was to make drivers "heros again"

# The radio ban timeline

## 2014: Announced and Enforced

- In the FIA's September 11 2014 directive, Charlie Whiting states: "No radio conversation from pit to driver may include any information that is related to the performance of the car or driver."
- This applied from Singapore, round 14 of the 2014 season, 21 9 2014
- This was implemented to enforce Article 20.1 of the sporting regulations: "The driver must drive the car alone and unaided."
	- I searched through the 2014 sporting regulations, and there was no mention about driver radios. 
	- Radio appears only in Articles 12.5, 34 and 40.1 and the podium ceremony rules, and none limits messages to drivers.
	- RaceFans and Formula1.com both give Article 20.1 as basis for the race ban 
	- I have not covered any amendments after 28-02-2014, the release date of the 2014 sporting regulations.
- Whiting also mentioned data transmission from pit to car is prohibited by Article 8.5.2 of the 2014 and 2015 technical regulations.
- Article 8.7 of the 2014,2015 and 2016 regulations, named "Driver radio," describes that a voice radio system between car and pits must be stand-alone and must not transmit or receive other data. All such communications must be open and accessible to the FIA and broadcasters. It has no mentions of driver radio coaching.

## 2014 Ban Revision

- This ban mainly focused on driver performance, including:
	- Braking points, a radio example being: "Try braking 10m earlier for Turn 8"
	- Racing lines, examples being "A wider line into Turn 12," "Take less kerb on the entry into Turn 10," "Li-Co (lift and coast) 50m before Turn 3", etc.
	- Gear selection, an example being "Try to downshift to 2nd instead of only 3rd for turn 16"
		- For gear selection, different sources state different things. The FIA postponed this, but Motorsport.com's 2016 analysis states that this was part of the 2014 ban.

## 2015
- There was an initial push to widen the ban to also restrict car performance coaching, yet it was postponed by the FIA.

## 2016
- The car performance coaching was enforced in the 2016 Australian Grand Prix, restricting any technical guidance from engineers
- The FIA eventually lifts the ban at the German Grand Prix the same year (31-07-2016 Round 12/21)
- FIA's statement at the German Grand Prix, as quoted by RaceFans: "With the exception of the period between the start of the formation lap and the start of the race, there will be no limitations on messages teams send to their drivers either by radio or pit board."
- It was framed by the FIA that it created better content for fans, because teams must give the commercial rights holder unrestricted access to the messages.
- The additional things that were banned:
	- car setup adjustments, examples being: "Brake Balance 2 clicks forwards;" "Switch engine mode to mode 4 for the next 5 laps;" "open differential on entry, minus 2"
	- direct technical questions, examples being: "how is the balance;" "any other engine modes that will stop my engine from overheating;" etc.

## Method
### Data 
- I will get the data from Jolpica, an F1 api that stores data for lap times, lap numbers, pit stops, driver positions, etc. 
- Data from 2014,2015 and 2016 will be pulled for all of the applicable driver pairs
- A function will be written and reused for every race that gets pulled to calculate the gap in median lap time

### Measuring the gap
Take one race and one driver pair. Find each driver's median race lap time, using clean laps only.

Driver A: 90.0 seconds
Driver B: 90.6 seconds

Subtract to get the absolute difference: 0.6 seconds, so that a positive value is always obtained
Divide by the faster driver's time: 0.6 / 90.0 = 0.0067. Set division to be the faster driver for a consistent standard, not just randomly choosing a driver everytime.
Multiply by 100: 0.67 percent.
This is called the % difference in median lap time
every pair gets one of these per race

### Cleaning the data
- Only include green flag laps to show real racing pace
- Drop lap 1 to avoid traffic and grid position variable
- Drop in pit laps and out pit laps as they will be slower due to the pitlane
- Drop safety car laps since drivers are mandated to go slower during a safety car
- Drop wet races, explained later

### Wet races
 - 2014 Hungary and Japan. 2015 Britain and USA. 2016 Monaco, Britain and Brazil were all wet races. That is 7 of 59, so 52 are dry.
- A Reddit list of wet-weather races was used for this. Reddit is user-compiled and weak as evidence.
- I checked all 59 races against their race pages (Wikipedia for 57 races, f1-fansite.com for the 2014 Australian and Malaysian races, see Links). The result matches the Reddit list on all 7 wet races.
- Japan 2014 and Brazil 2016 were wet throughout. Hungary 2014, USA 2015, Monaco 2016 and Britain 2016 started wet and dried out. Britain 2015 started dry and turned wet from about lap 33.
- I drop every wet race. This is because the lap data gives times and positions but no weather or tyre data for each lap, so I cannot tell wet laps from dry laps inside a mixed race. 
- Dropping the whole race also keeps the method simple.
- Wet races costed me 7 of 59 races, which is 12 percent. Britain 2015 loses about 30 dry laps, because rain began around lap 33.

### Statistics: The shift in median lap time gaps between change periods
- The shift is the average gap of median lap times of the banned group - the average gap of median lap times in the non banned group
- An example with made up numbers would be: no-ban gaps 0.20, 0.30 and 0.25 average 0.25. Banned gaps 0.35, 0.40 and 0.30 average 0.35. The shift is +0.10 percentage points.
- I get 2 shifts since the ban starts and ends. The first gap should be positive (gap widens) and the second gap should be negative (gap narrows).
- The 3 periods that gets compared:
	- Before: 2014 Rounds 1-13, 12 dry (Hungary 2014 removed)
	- During: 2014 Rounds 14-19, 2015 Rounds 1-21 and 2016 Rounds 1-11

### Statistics: The permutations test
- I use this test because it assumes nothing about the shape or labels of my data
- The test will only assume that if the ban did nothing, the ban labels could be swapped around without changing the shift
- I put the % difference in median lap time numbers of the 3 pairs in 1 pool, and randomly deal each % difference into a fake no ban group of 63 (21 races x 3 pirs) and a fake coaching ban group of 66 (.
- throw away their labels, and randomly assign those gap numbers to either the fake banned or fake non banned group. 
- Then I will calculate the shift for the fake groups.
- I repeat bullet points 1 and 2 10 000 times
- the p value is the share of shifts that give a shift as big as the real shift or bigger
- I would run the test separately for each of the 3 driver pairs, giving each pair their own p value for the races from 2014-2016
- I would take the gaps from the 3 pairs and pool them, then put each into a ban or no ban group, then run the tests.
- Pooling does have a weakness, if the gap between a pair is larger than the other pairs, then that gap will drive the results
this is the algorithm that i will use: 
```python
import numpy as np
legal = np.array([0.20, 0.30, 0.25])
banned = np.array([0.35, 0.40, 0.30])
observed = banned.mean() - legal.mean()
pooled = np.concatenate([legal, banned])
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
count = 0
for _ in range(10000):
    rng.shuffle(pooled)
    if pooled[:len(banned)].mean() - pooled[len(banned):].mean() >= observed - 1e-9:
        count += 1
print(observed, count / 10000)
```
